Smart supervision for small banks

Contribution by Frank Elderson, Member of the Executive Board of the ECB and Vice-Chair of the Supervisory Board of the ECB, for Börsen-Zeitung

Frankfurt am Main, 16 November 2026

Germany’s small and regional banks are trusted partners to the Mittelstand and households across the country, often drawing on deep-rooted relationships in their communities. They play an important role in financing the real economy and support jobs and investment in the regions they serve.

This reflects a broader European reality. Across Europe, a diverse banking sector – combining a range of business models, sizes and areas of expertise – helps ensure that households and businesses can access the financing they need.

As Europe searches for ways to strengthen its long-term growth and competitiveness, these banks will continue to play an important role. But they are very different from large, internationally active banking groups: their business models are often simpler, their activities more focused and their risk profiles less complex. A regulatory and supervisory framework that recognises those differences can support both resilience and competitiveness.

This is already partly the case. The EU prudential framework recognises the diversity of EU banks through the concept of proportionality: the idea that regulatory requirements and supervisory practices should reflect a bank’s size, complexity and risk profile, while maintaining high standards that protect depositors and the stability of our financial system.

This framework already covers a large share of Europe’s smaller banks. Today, around 75% of the banks supervised by national authorities in the euro area – more than 1,400 institutions – qualify as small and non-complex, and thus benefit from proportionate treatment. In Germany, that share is even higher: 81%. Among other things, these banks need only report up to 30% of the data reported by their larger counterparts. Supervisors also tailor the frequency and intensity of their oversight to the complexity and risk profile of each bank.

At the same time, experience gained from applying the framework over recent years has shown that some smaller banks still face regulatory requirements and supervisory practices that were originally designed with much larger and more complex institutions in mind. A more tailored approach would allow supervisors to focus on the risks that matter most while reducing unnecessary complexity for banks.

Over recent months, the ECB and national supervisors have examined where oversight can be simplified without compromising resilience. The result is a set of practical proposals aimed at making oversight for smaller banks more risk-focused, efficient and effective. Rather than creating a new framework, the proposals build on the one already in place for small and non-complex banks. The goal is simple: to preserve the same high standards of safety and soundness while reducing unnecessary complexity.

One important step would be to broaden the range of institutions eligible for the existing framework. To qualify today, a bank must have a balance sheet of less than €5 billion. But experience has shown that size alone does not necessarily make a bank more complex. In Germany, for example, a wave of consolidation among smaller and regional banks has created larger institutions without necessarily making their business models riskier or more complex. In fact, greater scale can help these banks benefit from lower cost-to-income ratios, become more efficient and invest in key areas like digitalisation and cybersecurity.

Amending the regulation to allow national authorities to raise the threshold to €10 billion, where warranted by the bank’s risk profile and the structure of its domestic banking sector, would therefore enable more banks to benefit from proportionate treatment.

Another key change would be to redefine what “non-complex” actually means. Today, some banks are excluded from the framework for largely technical reasons related to resolution planning, rather than reasons of genuine complexity.

Together, these regulatory changes would bring an additional 150 or so banks under the small and non-complex umbrella, making them eligible for more proportionate treatment.

But changing legislation is only one part of the solution. We also need to adapt the way supervision is carried out in practice, and we are already working to do just that.

For banks whose risk profile is stable and well understood, some supervisory exercises could be carried out less frequently or more efficiently. For example, supervisors could increasingly run stress test projections centrally rather than requiring banks to carry them out themselves. This would substantially lighten the workload for almost 1,000 institutions that currently need to do so. Reporting requirements are another area that could be simplified. To this end, together with the European Banking Authority we are pursuing reforms that should ultimately reduce reporting by small and non-complex banks to around 5% of the data points reported by large banks. This would allow both supervisors and banks to focus their efforts where risks are greatest, while reducing unnecessary burdens.

None of this changes a fundamental reality: smaller banks are not immune to risk.

Cyber threats are becoming more sophisticated. Geopolitical tensions create uncertainty. Digitalisation is transforming the financial sector. Climate and nature-related risks are increasingly affecting businesses and households, and the banks that finance them.

That is why greater proportionality cannot mean lower standards. Depositors in a small regional bank should have the same confidence in the safety of their savings as customers of a large international institution. That principle is paramount when considering changes to the framework for small and non-complex banks.

A stronger and more competitive Europe needs banks that make the most of their strengths. And across the continent, there is growing recognition that competitiveness and resilience go hand in hand. Well-designed regulation should support both.

For smaller banks, that means oversight that preserves high standards while recognising their specific characteristics. A more proportionate framework can help them remain resilient, direct scarce resources towards managing the risks that matter most, and focus on what they do best: helping local businesses and hidden champions thrive.
