Resilience in an age of shocks: lessons from central and eastern Europe

Speech by Boris Vujčić, Vice-President of the ECB, at the Conference on European Economic Integration (CEEI) 2026

Vienna, 24 November 2026

This conference has long been associated with one of Europe’s most remarkable economic achievements: the convergence of the countries of central and eastern Europe (CEE).[1]

Since the mid-1990s, incomes in the region have more than doubled and the gap with advanced European economies has narrowed substantially (slide 2). EU accession and deeper integration into the Single Market were powerful forces behind this convergence, with openness being central to that success. Integration into European and global value chains supported productivity, while foreign direct investment accelerated capital accumulation and technology transfer.[2]

Yet the same openness that enabled convergence has also exposed CEE countries to repeated shocks. In the space of just a few years, Europe has faced a pandemic, Russia’s unjustified war against Ukraine, an unprecedented energy shock, disruptions to global supply chains and, more recently, renewed trade tensions and conflict in the Middle East.

These developments have placed economic resilience at the centre of the policy debate. And central and eastern Europe provides a particularly useful lens through which to think about what resilience means.

The experience of CEE economies provides valuable lessons for Europe as a whole. It shows us that openness and resilience are not opposites. In fact, integration can be a source of resilience through diversifying markets, suppliers and sources of financing. The question then is how to preserve the benefits of integration while strengthening our capacity to withstand and adapt to shocks.

So what actually makes an economy resilient? Resilience is not about avoiding shocks altogether – no economy can do that. Nor does it mean trying to become self-sufficient. Rather, resilience means having the buffers, institutions and flexibility to absorb shocks when they arrive, the capacity to recover afterwards and, ultimately, the ability to adapt to the new environment.

Today I would like to consider four dimensions of that resilience: monetary, fiscal, financial and structural.

Monetary resilience: credible policy provides the anchor

Let me begin with monetary resilience.

The inflation shock that started in 2021 was an extraordinary test for central banks, reminding us that inflation can return unexpectedly and forcefully (slide 3).

And it was particularly significant in central and eastern Europe, where food and energy tend to weigh more heavily in household consumption baskets. Many economies in the region are also relatively energy-intensive, magnifying their exposure to the initial shock. Tight labour markets and strong wage growth subsequently contributed to domestic price pressures.[3]

Divergent inflation developments can require different monetary policy responses. For a small open economy outside the monetary union, this can also lead to exchange rate movements and shifts in capital flows and financing conditions, potentially amplifying the original shock.

Credible central banks can help contain these risks.

The experience of recent years reminds us why price stability is itself a source of resilience (slide 4). When inflation expectations remain anchored and people trust the central bank to fulfil its mandate, an external supply shock is less likely to turn into persistent inflation.

But credibility cannot be taken for granted. It has to be earned. And that requires central banks to act when circumstances demand it, even when the decisions involved are difficult.

There is also a broader lesson to be learnt from the region’s experience: monetary resilience depends on the monetary and financial structures in which economies operate.

Here, participation in the euro area can provide important benefits.

For a highly integrated economy, joining the euro area removes exchange rate risk from its interactions with its main trading and financial partners. It eliminates the possibility of currency movements amplifying a shock through balance sheets or financing conditions. And it gives the economy access to the institutions and common monetary framework of the euro area.

Croatia provides a useful illustration.

Before euro adoption, the Croatian economy, like many in the region, was already highly “euroised”, with a large share of private and public liabilities denominated in euro. Joining the euro therefore removed an important source of foreign currency risk and strengthened the economy’s resilience to financial shocks.[4]

This does not mean that participation in the euro area fully insulates countries from shocks. We know very well that it does not. But it does remove one potential source of instability: the risk that exchange rate movements amplify those shocks. In periods of heightened uncertainty, these benefits become particularly valuable.

And this illustrates my first lesson: monetary resilience ultimately rests on credible institutions and a stable nominal anchor.

Fiscal resilience: building buffers before they are needed

The second dimension is fiscal resilience.

The principle here is simple: buffers need to be built in good times, before they are needed during downturns.

Fiscal policy played a crucial stabilising role during the pandemic and again during the energy crisis, helping to cushion the impact of exceptionally large shocks on households and firms. Importantly, countries with stronger public finances had greater room to respond.

Many CEE economies still benefit from comparatively low levels of public debt. This is an important source of resilience, but it requires continued efforts if it is to be preserved (slide 5).

When we look at fiscal flows, budget deficits have increased across much of the region in recent years, reflecting a combination of higher social and investment spending, rising public sector wage bills, higher interest rate expenditure and, increasingly, greater defence spending.

At the same time, headline fiscal positions do not tell the whole story. The structure of public debt and the strength of fiscal institutions also matter. In some non-euro area CEE economies, foreign currency exposures and reliance on non-resident investors remain significant, while contingent liabilities and weaknesses in fiscal governance can create additional vulnerabilities.[5]

Credible and sound fiscal policies, supported by strong fiscal frameworks, are therefore essential to preserve confidence and fiscal space, and also to reduce the risk that changes in market sentiment translate into abrupt increases in sovereign financing costs.

And while euro area membership can reduce risks related to euro-denominated public debt, it is no substitute for sound fiscal policies. Maintaining adequate fiscal buffers remains essential, including within a monetary union.[6]

So the lesson is straightforward: relatively low debt is a valuable asset for many CEE economies, but it is an asset that needs to be preserved. Fiscal resilience requires both sufficient buffers and credible institutions that help safeguard them.

Financial resilience: absorbing shocks, integrating markets

This brings me to the third dimension: financial sector resilience.

Europe has made enormous progress since the global financial crisis (slide 6). Banks today hold much more capital and liquidity, and have weathered multiple shocks.

Central and eastern Europe is no exception: banks there are generally well capitalised and profitable, and non-performing loans are close to historical lows in most countries.

But we should not get too comfortable. Geopolitical tensions and renewed energy price pressures could weigh on firms’ profitability and debt servicing capacity, with effects on bank asset quality emerging only gradually.

Rapid credit growth and strong house price increases in several CEE economies also call for vigilance. When monetary policy is set at euro area level, sound macroprudential policy has a particularly important role in addressing country-specific cycles.

Crucially, resilience also depends on the quality of the frameworks and institutions, and nowhere is this more true than in the financial sector. The stronger regulatory and supervisory standards put in place after the global financial crisis remain the backbone of a resilient financial sector and a prerequisite for sustainable growth.

That said, there is scope to reduce undue complexities which may otherwise unnecessarily hamper the euro area financial sector’s competitiveness, without weakening capital requirements or compromising financial stability. The objective should be simpler regulation, not weaker safeguards.[7]

There is also a broader structural challenge. Financial systems in much of central and eastern Europe remain heavily bank-based, while capital markets and institutional investor bases are relatively shallow. This limits the range of funding available to firms and constrains the capacity of markets to absorb shocks. This is not only a challenge for central and eastern Europe, but a European challenge.

Integrated financial markets can help address this by broadening the pool of investors and sources of finance available to firms, and by allowing investors to diversify their portfolios across countries. When a shock hits one country, part of the loss is shared with investors abroad and financing from abroad can keep investment and credit going. In this way, market-based finance complements bank lending and reduces the extent to which domestic shocks translate into domestic financing constraints.[8]

This matters particularly for small, open CEE economies, whose domestic capital markets are not yet deep enough to provide substantial private risk-sharing on their own. Integration offers a double gain: it connects firms and investors to deeper European markets, and it helps domestic capital markets to grow and the financial system to diversify.

The path to deeper integration is therefore not simply about financing investment and supporting growth; it is also about strengthening the capacity of our economies to absorb shocks. Completing banking union and advancing the savings and investments union are essential steps along the way.

Structural resilience: adapting without turning inward

Let me turn finally to what I see as the new frontier of resilience: our ability to adapt to structural and geoeconomic change.

There are three areas that are particularly instructive for central and eastern Europe: energy, supply chains and demographics.

Energy provides perhaps the clearest example of how vulnerability can become a catalyst for adaptation.

Several CEE economies entered the current decade highly dependent on Russian fossil fuels and, in some cases, on individual pipeline routes (slide 7).

Russia’s unjustified invasion of Ukraine brutally exposed that vulnerability.[9]

But Europe adapted. Since the beginning of the war, Europe, and the CEE region in particular, have substantially reduced their reliance on Russian energy imports, through diversification, new infrastructure, alternative suppliers and adjustments in energy demand.

There is an important lesson we can draw from this. Resilience is not simply about avoiding exposure before a shock. It is also about how quickly you can adapt once the shock occurs.

And adaptation is a continuous process. The experience of recent years has accelerated diversification, strengthened energy interconnections and encouraged investment in domestic low-carbon energy sources, all of which can reduce vulnerability to future shocks.

A similar principle applies to supply chains.

Central and eastern Europe’s economic success has been built in large part on integration into European and global manufacturing networks. This should not be seen as a vulnerability that needs to be reversed. On the contrary, it has enabled firms in the region to reach beyond their relatively small domestic markets, while foreign direct investment has supported capital accumulation, technology transfer and productivity growth.

But the nature of the risks surrounding those networks has changed.

Global trade tensions are rising, while export restrictions on critical raw materials and technologies have become an increasingly important tool of geoeconomic policy. Global competition, especially from China, is also increasing in manufacturing sectors that have traditionally been important sources of European competitiveness, including automotive industries, machinery and electrical equipment.[10] At the same time, geopolitical tensions can disrupt key trade routes and increase the costs and uncertainty associated with complex global value chains.

These risks are particularly relevant for central and eastern Europe. Several economies in the region are important centres of European industrial production in their own right. Their deep integration into cross-border production networks is a source of competitiveness, but it also means that they are exposed both to disruptions propagating along supply chains and to intensifying competitive pressures in key manufacturing sectors.

Therefore, the right response is not decoupling or self-sufficiency, but greater diversification and stronger capacity for innovation.

Or, to echo what I said earlier, openness and resilience can go together.

That means identifying critical dependencies, diversifying suppliers where economically feasible and strengthening the Single Market. It also means building more diversified and adaptable value chains, so that firms can respond when individual suppliers, transport routes or markets are disrupted. And it means investing in innovation, technology and skills, so that firms can adapt to changing patterns of global competition and shift to higher-value-added activities.

Pulling back from global networks would not necessarily reduce risk. Fewer connections can simply mean more concentrated links, leaving us with less diversification and fewer opportunities for innovation and productivity growth, and, ultimately, a lower standard of living.

There is one final structural challenge I would like to highlight, and it may ultimately be the most persistent for central and eastern Europe: demographics.

Unlike an energy shock or a financial market correction, demographic change moves slowly (slide 8).

But that does not make it less consequential. Ageing populations and labour shortages are increasingly important constraints on economic growth in Europe.

And across much of central and eastern Europe, population ageing is being compounded by the legacy of strong outward migration of young and skilled workers, as well as shrinking working-age populations.

But migration patterns have changed markedly in recent years. Many CEE economies have shifted from net emigration towards net immigration, supported by rising wage convergence, return migration and inflows of non-EU workers. Since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the arrival of Ukrainian refugees has reinforced this shift.

These inflows are already helping economies adjust to demographic pressures. Migrants have helped fill labour shortages in sectors where vacancies have been particularly high, including manufacturing, construction, transport and accommodation services.

Poland provides a striking recent example. Ukrainian refugees have been effectively integrated into the labour market, expanding labour supply and supporting economic activity.[11]

More broadly, immigration can boost incomes through investment and capital deepening, while high-skilled migration can also contribute to human capital and productivity.[12]

The broader lesson is that demographic pressures need not translate one-for-one into weaker economic growth. We cannot change demographic structures overnight, but resilience can still be strengthened by policies that raise participation, invest in skills, facilitate mobility and successfully integrate migrants.

Conclusion: resilience through openness and adaptation

What, then, does central and eastern Europe teach us about resilience?

First, monetary resilience. Credible institutions and price stability provide an anchor when shocks arrive, while being part of the euro area can remove an important source of instability.

Second, fiscal resilience. Relatively low public debt is a valuable asset for many CEE economies and it needs to be preserved. Building and maintaining fiscal buffers in good times creates room to respond when shocks occur.

Third, financial resilience. Strong banks and sound rules help economies absorb shocks rather than amplify them. Deeper European financial integration can help share risks more widely across borders.

And fourth, structural resilience. This means being able to manage strategic dependencies and adapt to structural shifts: diversifying energy sources and supply chains, investing in innovation and skills, and responding to demographic changes by making the most of the labour force available to us.
